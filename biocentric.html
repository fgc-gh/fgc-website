What is biocentrism?

The living world matters for its own sake, not only for what it gives people. We call this biocentrism.

The claim

Biocentrism is the view that every living thing has a good of its own, and that this good deserves moral consideration. A salmon, an oak, a lichen on a rock: each can do well or badly, be helped or harmed. That fact gives us reasons to care about how our actions affect it, whether or not it is useful to us.

Most of our laws and economics start from a different place. They treat the living world as a set of resources and services, valuable to the extent that people want or need them. Biocentrism does not deny that nature is useful. It denies that usefulness is the whole story.

What it does not say

Biocentrism is often confused with positions it does not hold. Clearing those up answers many objections before they start.

  • It does not say all life is equal in every respect. A person and a mosquito both have a good of their own. That does not make every conflict between them a tie. It means the mosquito's good counts for something, so harming it needs a reason.
  • It does not say people don't matter. People are living beings too. Biocentrism widens the circle of moral concern; it does not push anyone out of it.
  • It does not say we can never use or harm other beings. Every animal, including us, eats, builds, and takes up space. The question is which harms are justified, and how much harm we accept for what reasons.
  • It does not rest on mysticism. The core claim needs no belief in spirits, a world soul, or nature as a person. It rests on what biology tells us about how living things work.

Why believe it

Start with something no biologist disputes. Living things are not like rocks or rivers. An organism builds and repairs itself, draws energy from its surroundings, responds to threats, and works to keep going and to reproduce. Biologists call this goal-directed organization. It is why we can say, plainly and without metaphor, that a tree is thriving or dying, that drought is bad for it, that sunlight is good for it.

That is all "having a good of its own" means. It does not require the tree to think or feel. A tree does not need to know it is dying for dying to be bad for it, just as an unconscious person can still be harmed.

Now consider why harming people is wrong. Most ethical traditions point to the same thing: a person's life can go well or badly for them, and we should not damage it without good reason. If that is the reason, it is hard to say why it stops at the edge of our species. Other beings also have lives that can go well or badly for them. Drawing the moral line exactly around humans, and treating everything else as raw material, needs a justification. The burden is on the person drawing that line, not on the person who declines to.

The usual candidates for that line, such as reason, language, or the ability to make agreements, do not hold up well. Some people lack them, and we do not think those people may be used as resources. And none of these traits explains why lacking them would make a being's suffering or death not count at all.

Answering the skeptic

"Value only exists if someone values it."

This mixes up two things: being valued and having a good. Whether anyone values a forest is a fact about people. Whether the forest's trees can be harmed is a fact about the trees. Biocentrism makes the second claim. It says we have reasons to take that good into account, the same way a doctor has reasons to care for a patient who is unconscious and cannot value anything at that moment.

"This is just sentimental. You're projecting feelings onto plants."

No feelings are required. The claim is that plants and other organisms have interests in the plain biological sense: conditions that help or hinder them. Foresters, farmers, and ecologists talk this way every day. Biocentrism only asks us to take seriously what that language already admits.

"If everything matters, you can't make any decisions."

Medicine faces the same problem: every patient matters, and doctors still triage. Biocentric ethics has developed principles for conflicts. Self-defense is allowed. Meeting basic needs takes priority over luxuries. When harm can't be avoided, choose the option that does the least. Where harm is done, try to make it right. These principles do not make hard cases easy, but they make them decidable, and they rule out a great deal of harm that is done today for convenience or profit.

"Human needs have to come first."

Often they should, when they are real needs. Biocentrism has no quarrel with feeding people or sheltering them. Its quarrel is with treating every human want, however trivial, as automatically outweighing any harm to other life. Clearcutting an old forest for cheap lumber, or draining a wetland for a parking lot, is not a case of human survival against nature. It is a case of convenience against life.

"Nature is brutal. Predators kill all the time."

Ethics is about choices made by beings who can make them. A wolf can't weigh its options or change its diet; we can. Biocentrism asks how we should act, not how wolves should.

"Protecting nature for people's sake works fine. Why add this?"

Because arguments from human benefit can always be outbid. If a river matters only for its value to people, then a dam that is worth more to people wins, and the river loses. Species with no known use, places no one visits, and harms that fall on the far future all lose these calculations again and again. A view that gives living things standing of their own is not only more accurate. It is also harder to trade away.

What changes in practice

Taking biocentrism seriously changes the questions we ask about any project, plan, or policy:

  • Who, besides people, will be harmed, and how badly?
  • Is the harm needed to meet a real need, or only to save money or time?
  • Is there a less harmful way to do it?
  • Who carries the burden of proof? We think it should fall on those who want to cause harm, not on the living world to prove its worth.

We also think honesty requires saying that the current economic system is built to answer these questions the other way. Many proposed fixes change the fuel source or the accounting while keeping the assumption that the world is raw material. We would rather end destructive extraction than make it slightly more efficient.

What it means for our work

For Fertile Ground Conservancy, this view shapes which projects we sponsor, which campaigns we help, and what we turn down. We support work that treats the beings and communities of life in a place as having standing of their own. We don't support projects that trade away ecological health to be politically easier, or that treat people as the only beings who matter.

This is a demanding standard, and we don't always meet it. But we think it is the right one, and we think it can be defended on its merits.

Further reading

  • Respect for NaturePaul W. Taylor, 1986

    The most rigorous philosophical defense of biocentric ethics, including how to weigh competing claims.

  • Braiding SweetgrassRobin Wall Kimmerer, 2013

    Indigenous and scientific ways of knowing, woven into an ethic of reciprocity.

  • The Spell of the SensuousDavid Abram, 1996

    How the more-than-human world is alive and communicating.

  • Deep Ecology for the 21st CenturyGeorge Sessions, editor, 1995

    Essays defining and defending a biocentric view of nature.

  • A Sand County AlmanacAldo Leopold, 1949

    The land ethic. Still one of the most important things written about our duties to the living world.
