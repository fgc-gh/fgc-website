Why It Matters

Coho salmon don't know what a scoping notice is. The spotted owl doesn't know that a comment period closed last Tuesday. The Port Orford cedar on the ridge has no way of knowing that an environmental assessment was completed, found no significant impact, and authorized the road that will bring the pathogen that kills it. None of this is visible to the beings most affected by it.

Most wild places aren't lost in the field. They're lost on paper — in administrative proceedings that move faster than the communities they threaten can mobilize, in legal records that get built by default when no one shows up to contest them, in permits that pass review because the burden of proof was never placed on the agency. By the time the machines arrive, the decision is final. The window for changing it closed months ago.

What ecodefense does is show up before that moment. It builds the administrative record while the window is open. It names the species, cites the applicable statutes, surfaces the cumulative effects the agency preferred not to address, and makes the analytical case the agency is legally required to respond to. This is not protest. It is participation in the actual process by which these decisions get made — on behalf of the beings whose interests are otherwise unrepresented in that process.

The problem is throughput. The volume of administrative actions threatening ecologically significant landscapes vastly exceeds the capacity of small organizations to track, analyze, and respond. Most threats pass without challenge — not because the work is impossible but because no one has the bandwidth to do it. We are solving that problem.

Conservation Defense Initiative

The Conservation Defense Initiative is FGC's answer to the throughput problem. It is infrastructure for systematic, repeatable ecodefense — designed to let a small organization be present in the administrative record at a volume that would otherwise require a staff of twenty.

The system works in four stages. Automated monitoring tracks public agency document repositories — USFS NEPA trackers, BLM NEPA registers, Oregon DLCD, California CEQA portals — and pulls new filings as they are posted. Each document is parsed, classified, and scored against ecological watch criteria; documents that cross the threshold trigger an alert. For flagged actions, a retrieval-augmented drafting system grounded in our archive of prior interventions, applicable case law, species recovery plans, and regional land management documents generates a first-draft comment. Every intervention passes through human review before submission.

The result: a small organization can be present in dozens of proceedings simultaneously, each one with a substantive comment grounded in ecological science and environmental law, each one building the administrative record that later challenge depends on.

CDI is designed to be replicable. The architecture is geography-configurable. The knowledge base is structured for sharing. Other organizations facing the same throughput problem will be able to adapt and deploy it.

Get Involved

This work depends on people with specific skills. If any of the following describes you, we want to hear from you:

  • Legal expertise in environmental or administrative law
  • Wildlife biology, hydrology, or ecology with knowledge of Cascadia's landscapes
  • Experience in NEPA, CEQA, or other environmental review processes
  • Ability to write clearly and precisely about ecology, land use, or environmental policy
  • Organizing experience that bridges administrative advocacy and public campaigning

We operate on the unceded territories of the Tolowa, Yurok, Karuk, and other Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Coast. These nations have lived in constitutive relationship with this land for thousands of years — not managing it, not owning it, but belonging to it in a way that shaped their language, governance, ceremony, and body of knowledge. What FGC is trying to practice, they have been practicing since long before conservation was a word. We are working to act in a manner consistent with that prior claim, and we fall short of it regularly. We name this not as ceremony but as accountability.
